# Storytelling Skill

## 1. Purpose

This skill teaches and applies the craft of storytelling.

Its job is to turn a subject, idea, experience, or set of supplied material into a clear, compelling narrative that holds attention and leaves the audience with a meaningful understanding or feeling.

This is a storytelling skill only. It does not define research, source discovery, fact-checking, project orchestration, agent delegation, production management, publishing, or workflow ownership. Those responsibilities belong to other skills or agents.

The storytelling agent focuses on:
- Finding the human, intellectual, emotional, or practical heart of a subject.
- Identifying the question that makes the audience care.
- Choosing the strongest narrative angle.
- Designing a coherent story structure.
- Creating hooks, scenes, tension, curiosity, reveals, and satisfying payoffs.
- Making complex ideas understandable through narrative.
- Writing natural, vivid, speakable narration and dialogue.
- Using visual storytelling when the medium is video.
- Revising for clarity, momentum, emotional effect, and meaning.

## 2. When to use this skill

Use this skill when asked to:
- Develop a story from a topic, idea, premise, or supplied material.
- Find a compelling angle for a documentary or explainer.
- Create a logline, premise, central question, story promise, outline, beat sheet, or scene list.
- Write or improve a documentary, educational, historical, science, biography, or explainer script.
- Strengthen a hook, opening, transition, reveal, emotional arc, or ending.
- Improve pacing, suspense, clarity, voice, dialogue, or audience engagement.
- Convert an information-heavy explanation into a narrative.
- Critique a draft and recommend concrete storytelling revisions.
- Adapt a story for voiceover, video, podcast, speech, article, or short-form content.

Do not expand the task beyond what was requested. If the user asks for an outline, deliver an outline; if they ask for a script, deliver a script.

## 3. Default audience and voice

Follow the user's brief whenever it gives a different direction.

For a faceless YouTube documentary or explainer:
- Audience: curious general viewers with no assumed specialist knowledge.
- Voice: intelligent, conversational, clear, vivid, and natural when spoken aloud.
- Tone: intriguing and cinematic without becoming melodramatic, exaggerated, or artificially mysterious.
- Complexity: explain unfamiliar ideas in plain language while preserving the important nuance.
- Momentum: give the audience a reason to keep listening at every major stage.
- Visuals: make visual choices support the story rather than decorate it.
- Ending: deliver a meaningful payoff that fulfils the opening's promise.

Do not confuse “cinematic” with excessive adjectives, dramatic pauses, grand claims, or vague poetic language. Strong storytelling comes from meaningful events, specific details, contrast, causality, and change.

## 4. The foundations of a story

### 4.1 Subject versus story

A subject is what the content is about. A story is what happens, changes, is discovered, or comes into conflict around that subject.

Examples:

- Subject: the computer mouse.
- Story direction: how a device built to solve a specific interaction problem became an almost invisible part of modern computing.

- Subject: bananas.
- Story direction: how a familiar fruit travelled through trade, agriculture, science, and culture to become globally ordinary.

- Subject: the three-meal routine.
- Story direction: how a pattern that feels natural came to be shaped by work, culture, economics, and changing daily life.

A list of facts, dates, features, or definitions is not automatically a story. Look for a central question, a change, a tension, a causal chain, or a surprising connection.

### 4.2 The six story ingredients

A strong narrative often contains:
1. **Subject** — a person, group, object, system, place, or idea the audience can follow.
2. **Desire or question** — what someone wants, what needs solving, or what the audience wants to understand.
3. **Pressure** — an obstacle, contradiction, risk, limitation, uncertainty, or competing force.
4. **Movement** — actions, decisions, discoveries, experiments, failures, or developments.
5. **Consequence** — what changes because of those events.
6. **Meaning** — why the outcome matters beyond the sequence of events.

Not every story needs a conventional hero or villain. An object, invention, habit, institution, or scientific idea can function as the central subject.

### 4.3 Central story question

Write one clear question that the audience will want answered.

A useful central question:
- Is specific rather than generic.
- Creates curiosity without misleading the audience.
- Can sustain the intended length.
- Gives the story a direction.
- Can be answered meaningfully by the ending.

Weak: “What is the history of the computer mouse?”

Stronger: “How did a small experimental pointing device become one of the most familiar tools in computing?”

Do not force a question onto a topic if another narrative form is more natural. A character journey, transformation, mystery, comparison, or cause-and-effect narrative may work better.

### 4.4 Story promise

Write one sentence describing what the audience will understand, discover, or feel by the end.

The opening creates the promise. The middle develops it. The ending fulfils it.

Never open with a dramatic question or claim that the story does not meaningfully answer.

### 4.5 The story's change

Before writing, identify what changes:
- A character's circumstances, belief, goal, or relationship.
- The audience's understanding of an ordinary object.
- The role of an invention or custom in society.
- The balance of power between competing forces.
- The meaning of an event after new context is revealed.

If nothing changes, the script may be an explanation or catalogue rather than a developed narrative. That can still be useful, but do not pretend it has a dramatic arc.

## 5. Choosing the narrative angle

A topic can support several stories. Choose one angle rather than trying to tell every possible story at once.

Common narrative angles:

- **Origin:** Where did it begin, and what problem or condition produced it?
- **Transformation:** How did it become what we recognise today?
- **Journey:** How did it move through places, people, industries, or generations?
- **Problem and solution:** What challenge existed, and how was it addressed?
- **Failure and recovery:** What went wrong, and what changed afterward?
- **Competition:** What opposing ideas, products, people, or systems struggled for influence?
- **Hidden system:** What unseen process makes an ordinary experience possible?
- **Misconception:** What do people commonly assume, and what is more interesting?
- **Consequence:** What unexpected effects followed from a decision or invention?
- **Personal or human lens:** How did a larger development affect an individual's life?

Evaluate each possible angle using:
1. Curiosity — would someone want to know what happens next?
2. Clarity — can the angle be explained in one sentence?
3. Narrative movement — does it contain progression or change?
4. Depth — can it sustain the intended runtime without padding?
5. Payoff — does it lead to a satisfying final understanding?

Choose the angle with the strongest combination. Do not choose an angle merely because it sounds sensational.

## 6. Story structure

Structure is a guide for controlling information and attention, not a rigid formula. Choose the structure that best serves the story.

### 6.1 Three-act structure

**Act 1 — Setup**
- Introduce the subject and the audience's point of entry.
- Establish the central question or problem.
- Show why the question matters.
- Create an expectation that the story will develop.

**Act 2 — Development**
- Explore attempts, discoveries, obstacles, contradictions, or turning points.
- Increase the significance of the central question.
- Introduce complications that change the audience's understanding.
- Avoid repeating the same point in different words.

**Act 3 — Resolution**
- Bring the central question to a meaningful answer.
- Show the consequences of the story's events.
- Connect the ending to the opening.
- Leave the audience with a clear final insight or image.

### 6.2 The story spine

Use this simple chain to test whether the narrative has movement:

1. At first...
2. The situation changes when...
3. Because of that...
4. But then...
5. This forces...
6. Eventually...
7. As a result...

The links should express cause and consequence, not just chronological order.

### 6.3 Cause-and-effect structure

Use this when the subject is an invention, social habit, historical development, or system.

Build the chain:
- Initial condition.
- Pressure or problem.
- Response or invention.
- Immediate consequence.
- New problem or limitation.
- Adaptation or turning point.
- Wider effect.
- Present-day meaning.

Avoid implying that history was inevitable. Show where choices, circumstances, and alternatives mattered.

### 6.4 Mystery and discovery structure

Use this when the audience begins with an incomplete understanding.

1. Present an intriguing observation.
2. Establish what is puzzling about it.
3. Offer a plausible first explanation.
4. Reveal information that complicates that explanation.
5. Reframe the question.
6. Deliver the deeper explanation.
7. Show why the answer matters.

Do not withhold basic information simply to manufacture suspense. Every delay should create a meaningful change in understanding.

### 6.5 Parallel or contrast structure

Use this to compare two people, inventions, eras, approaches, or outcomes.

- Establish the comparison clearly.
- Identify the shared problem or context.
- Show the meaningful differences.
- Alternate between the subjects only when the comparison benefits.
- Bring the strands together in a conclusion that explains what the contrast reveals.

### 6.6 Chronological structure

Use chronology when the order of events is itself important or naturally creates escalation.

Chronology alone is not a story. Each period or event must change the situation, introduce a new pressure, or alter the audience's understanding.

### 6.7 Circular structure

Begin with an image, question, object, or situation that returns at the end with a different meaning.

The final return should show change. Repeating the opening without adding meaning is not a payoff.

## 7. Hooks and openings

The hook earns the audience's attention. It should create a specific reason to continue.

### 7.1 Useful hook types

- **Curiosity gap:** Present something familiar with an unexplained detail.
- **Unexpected contrast:** Put two ideas together that seem incompatible.
- **Consequential moment:** Begin at a decision or event that changes what follows.
- **Concrete scene:** Place the audience inside a vivid moment.
- **Provocative question:** Ask a question the story can genuinely answer.
- **Striking fact:** Use a meaningful fact that changes how the subject is perceived.
- **Present-to-past bridge:** Start with something familiar today, then reveal its less familiar past.
- **Outcome-first opening:** Show the consequence before explaining how it happened.

### 7.2 Hook construction

A practical opening sequence:
1. **Attention:** Give the audience a concrete image, contrast, question, or moment.
2. **Orientation:** Make clear what the subject is.
3. **Tension:** Reveal the puzzle, conflict, or consequence.
4. **Promise:** Suggest what the audience will understand by continuing.

The opening should be concise. Do not spend too long announcing that the story is interesting.

### 7.3 Hook rules

- Be specific.
- Create curiosity that the story can satisfy.
- Avoid generic openings such as “Since the beginning of time...”
- Avoid empty phrases such as “You won't believe what happened next.”
- Do not exaggerate stakes.
- Do not stack several rhetorical questions.
- Do not explain the entire story in the first paragraph.
- Do not hide the subject so long that the audience becomes confused.

Write several different openings when useful, then choose the one that best expresses the story rather than the one that sounds loudest.

## 8. Character, desire, and stakes

### 8.1 Character

A character is not defined only by biography. The audience follows a character because they want something, face pressure, make choices, and experience consequences.

When developing a character, consider:
- What do they want?
- Why do they want it?
- What stands in their way?
- What can they do about it?
- What choice reveals who they are?
- What changes for them?

For documentary and educational scripts, a character may be:
- An inventor or decision-maker.
- A user affected by a system.
- A group with a shared goal.
- A community adapting to change.
- A person whose experience makes an abstract issue concrete.

Do not invent private thoughts, dialogue, motives, or experiences. If a character's inner life is not known, describe observable actions or clearly frame an interpretation as such.

### 8.2 Desire

Desire gives a character direction. It can be practical, emotional, intellectual, social, or moral.

Examples:
- Make a machine easier to use.
- Prove an idea can work.
- Protect a livelihood.
- Gain recognition.
- Solve a daily frustration.
- Preserve a tradition.

### 8.3 Conflict

Conflict is the force that makes progress difficult. It does not require an antagonist.

Types of conflict:
- Person versus person.
- Person versus society.
- Person versus nature.
- Person versus technology.
- Person versus institution.
- Person versus time.
- Person versus self.
- Idea versus idea.
- Need versus limitation.

A strong narrative may contain several forms of conflict, but the central conflict should remain understandable.

### 8.4 Stakes

Stakes answer: “What changes or what is lost if this does not work?”

Types:
- Physical: safety, survival, health.
- Emotional: trust, belonging, identity.
- Social: reputation, status, acceptance.
- Financial: money, work, resources.
- Intellectual: a belief, theory, or assumption.
- Cultural: a practice, tradition, or shared identity.
- Practical: time, effort, convenience, access.

Scale the stakes honestly. A small story does not need world-changing consequences. Specific, relatable stakes are often stronger than inflated ones.

## 9. Scenes and storytelling detail

### 9.1 Scene versus summary

A **scene** lets the audience experience an event as it unfolds through action, setting, and detail.

A **summary** compresses time or explains what happened across a longer period.

Use scenes for:
- Important decisions.
- Turning points.
- Moments of discovery.
- Conflict.
- Consequences.
- Emotionally significant interactions.

Use summary for:
- Long stretches of routine.
- Background context.
- Repeated events.
- Transitions across time.
- Information that does not need to be dramatised.

A script should balance scenes and summary. Too much scene slows the story; too much summary makes it feel like a lecture.

### 9.2 Scene construction

A useful scene contains:
1. **Place and time:** Where and when are we?
2. **Immediate objective:** What is someone trying to do?
3. **Action:** What happens?
4. **Obstacle or uncertainty:** What makes the moment difficult or interesting?
5. **Turn:** What changes during the scene?
6. **Exit:** What question or consequence carries us forward?

Not every scene needs all six elements explicitly, but every scene should have a purpose.

### 9.3 Specific detail

Prefer details that help the audience picture, understand, or feel something.

Weak: “The machine was very complicated.”

Stronger: “The operator had to adjust three separate controls before the machine would respond.”

Specificity should clarify rather than decorate. Do not add sensory details that are unsupported or irrelevant.

### 9.4 Show, then explain

When useful:
1. Show an action, object, moment, or consequence.
2. Let the audience notice what is unusual.
3. Explain the underlying idea.
4. Connect the explanation to the larger story.

Do not force every abstract concept into a dramatic scene. Some ideas are best explained directly.

## 10. Exposition and explanation

Exposition gives the audience the information needed to understand the story.

### 10.1 The information ladder

Introduce information in this order when appropriate:
1. Familiar reference.
2. New concept.
3. Plain-language explanation.
4. Concrete example.
5. Why it matters to the current story.

Do not begin with specialist terminology when a familiar explanation can establish the idea first.

### 10.2 One new idea at a time

When explaining a complex subject:
- Break it into small conceptual steps.
- Define necessary terms when they first matter.
- Use comparisons only when they genuinely clarify.
- Explain the relationship between ideas.
- Pause the narrative briefly if the audience needs orientation.
- Return to the central story question after the explanation.

### 10.3 Analogy

A useful analogy maps an unfamiliar idea onto a familiar one.

Rules:
- Explain the shared feature.
- Make clear where the comparison stops being accurate.
- Do not let an analogy replace the real explanation.
- Avoid analogies that introduce more confusion than they remove.

### 10.4 Exposition through action

Whenever possible, let the audience learn through:
- A person trying something.
- A device being used.
- A system responding.
- A problem appearing.
- A comparison between two outcomes.

Use direct explanation when action would be artificial or inefficient.

## 11. Suspense, curiosity, and reveals

### 11.1 Suspense

Suspense is anticipation under uncertainty. The audience understands what is at stake but does not yet know the outcome.

Create suspense by:
- Establishing a clear objective.
- Showing what could go wrong.
- Making the obstacle understandable.
- Letting consequences build.
- Delaying the outcome only while the delay adds meaning.

### 11.2 Curiosity

Curiosity is the desire to close a gap in understanding.

Useful curiosity gaps:
- How did this happen?
- Why did this choice matter?
- What is missing from the familiar explanation?
- Why does this ordinary thing work this way?
- What changed between then and now?

Resolve questions deliberately. Do not create more open questions than the ending can answer.

### 11.3 Reveals

A reveal changes the audience's understanding of information they already have.

A strong reveal:
- Is surprising but understandable in retrospect.
- Reframes earlier details.
- Advances the central story.
- Feels earned rather than arbitrary.

Plant the necessary context before the reveal. A reveal should not depend on information the audience had no fair opportunity to understand.

### 11.4 Reversals and turning points

A turning point changes the direction of the story.

Examples:
- A plan fails.
- A new obstacle appears.
- An assumption proves incomplete.
- A discovery changes the goal.
- A character makes an irreversible choice.
- A solution creates a new problem.

Use turning points to create progression, not merely to add drama.

## 12. Pacing and audience retention

Pacing is the rate at which the audience receives information, experiences change, and moves toward the payoff.

### 12.1 Pacing principles

- Alternate explanation with action, example, contrast, or consequence.
- Vary sentence length and paragraph rhythm.
- Remove repeated information.
- Give important ideas enough space to land.
- Move quickly through predictable material.
- Slow down at meaningful decisions, discoveries, or emotional moments.
- Use transitions that create forward movement.
- Make each section change the audience's understanding.

### 12.2 The section-level test

At the end of every section, ask:
- What does the audience know now that they did not know before?
- What changed?
- What new question has emerged?
- Why should they continue?
- Does the next section follow naturally?

If a section only repeats or decorates an earlier point, cut or redesign it.

### 12.3 Retention without manipulation

Do not use constant cliffhangers, fake urgency, or misleading teases. Retention should come from genuine narrative progression.

A good transition may:
- Answer one question and introduce a deeper one.
- Show the consequence of the previous event.
- Reveal a contradiction.
- Shift from an individual example to a wider pattern.
- Move from the familiar present into an important past event.

### 12.4 Runtime

Estimate runtime using the actual narration and delivery style. A rough English voiceover planning range is around 130–160 words per minute, but this is not a fixed rule. Pauses, pronunciation, music, visual holds, and language affect duration.

For the final timing estimate, use a read-aloud test rather than relying only on word count.

## 13. Voice, narration, and dialogue

### 13.1 Write for the ear

Voiceover is heard once in real time. It should be easy to understand without rereading.

- Prefer clear sentence structures.
- Keep one main idea in each sentence.
- Put important information near the beginning.
- Use natural contractions when appropriate to the voice.
- Avoid long chains of subordinate clauses.
- Read difficult sentences aloud and simplify them.
- Use punctuation to support natural delivery, not to imitate every breath.

### 13.2 Sentence rhythm

Use variation:
- Short sentences for emphasis or impact.
- Medium sentences for explanation.
- Longer sentences when a thought needs to unfold smoothly.

Do not make every sentence short. Constant fragments sound mechanical. Do not make every sentence long. Dense narration becomes difficult to follow.

### 13.3 Voice consistency

Define the narrator's voice through:
- Vocabulary.
- Sentence length.
- Degree of formality.
- Humour.
- Emotional distance.
- Use of questions.
- Relationship with the audience.

Keep the voice consistent unless a deliberate shift serves the story.

### 13.4 Dialogue

Dialogue should reveal character, conflict, intention, or change.

- Give each speaker a distinct purpose.
- Avoid dialogue that only delivers exposition.
- Keep exchanges concise when the medium is voiceover-led.
- Use subtext when appropriate: what a character means may differ from what they say.
- Never fabricate quotations and present them as real speech.
- If dialogue is reconstructed or illustrative, label it clearly according to the project's conventions.

### 13.5 Narrator presence

The narrator can guide, question, interpret, compare, or react. The narrator should not constantly announce how fascinating everything is.

Use first person only when it strengthens the relationship with the audience or clarifies the perspective.

## 14. Visual storytelling for video

Visual storytelling is not a list of things to show while narration plays. The image should add information, emotion, orientation, scale, or contrast.

### 14.1 The visual purpose test

For every major visual, ask:
- What does the audience learn from seeing this?
- Does it make an abstract idea concrete?
- Does it establish time or place?
- Does it show action or consequence?
- Does it create a useful contrast?
- Does it reinforce the emotional meaning?

If the visual adds nothing, remove it or rethink it.

### 14.2 Visual methods

Choose the visual method that best communicates the idea:
- Real-world footage.
- Archival imagery.
- Photographs and documents.
- Maps and geographic movement.
- Timelines.
- Diagrams and labelled illustrations.
- Animated mechanisms or processes.
- Data visualisations.
- Reconstructed scenes.
- Metaphorical imagery.
- On-screen text for essential names, dates, or concepts.

Do not use an elaborate animation when a simple diagram would be clearer.

### 14.3 Visual and narration relationship

Use one of these relationships:
- **Complement:** The image adds context to the narration.
- **Demonstrate:** The image shows exactly how something works.
- **Contrast:** The image intentionally differs from the spoken idea.
- **Reveal:** The image gives the audience new information.
- **Emotional echo:** The image deepens the feeling without repeating the words.
- **Orient:** The image clarifies location, time, scale, or sequence.

Avoid literal repetition in which the narration says exactly what the image already communicates.

### 14.4 Visual script format

For video scripts, use a two-column or clearly labelled format when it helps production:

| Narration / Audio | Visual / On-screen direction |
|---|---|
| Spoken line or sound cue | What the viewer sees and what the visual communicates |

Visual directions should be specific enough to understand the intention, but should not become unnecessary technical production instructions.

## 15. Transitions and continuity

A transition connects two moments, ideas, or sections.

Useful transition types:
- **Causal:** “That decision created a different problem.”
- **Temporal:** “A few years later, the situation had changed.”
- **Contrast:** “But the solution had a limitation.”
- **Scale shift:** Move from one person's experience to a wider pattern.
- **Question:** Raise the next question that naturally follows.
- **Image match:** Return to an object or image with a new meaning.
- **Consequence:** Show what happened because of the previous event.

Avoid repetitive signposting such as “But that's not all,” “Now let's go back,” or “And this is where things get interesting.”

Continuity means the audience always understands:
- Where they are in the story.
- What they are learning.
- Why the current section follows the previous one.
- What question or expectation is carrying them forward.

## 16. Themes and meaning

A theme is the larger idea a story explores. It is not necessarily a lesson stated directly.

Possible themes:
- Convenience has hidden costs.
- Progress is shaped by unintended consequences.
- Ordinary habits have histories.
- A small design choice can change behaviour.
- Technology changes how people relate to the world.
- Familiarity can hide complexity.

A theme should emerge from the story's events and consequences. Avoid forcing a moral that the narrative does not support.

## 17. Endings and payoffs

The ending should feel both earned and meaningful.

### 17.1 Types of ending

- **Answer:** Resolve the central question.
- **Callback:** Return to the opening image or idea with changed meaning.
- **Reframing:** Show that the audience should now understand the subject differently.
- **Consequence:** Reveal what the events led to.
- **Reflection:** Connect the specific story to a larger human idea.
- **Open-ended:** Leave a genuine uncertainty when the subject does not have a settled answer.

### 17.2 Ending structure

A practical ending:
1. Resolve the central story question.
2. Show the consequence or significance.
3. Return to the opening promise.
4. End on one clear thought, image, or emotional note.

### 17.3 Ending rules

- Do not introduce a major new topic at the last moment.
- Do not repeat the entire script.
- Avoid generic moral lessons.
- Do not explain the meaning after the story has already made it clear.
- Do not add a call to action that damages the emotional ending.
- Make the final line specific to this story.

## 18. Revision and story editing

Revision is where the narrative becomes clear and purposeful.

### 18.1 Four revision passes

**Pass 1 — Structure**
- Is the central question clear?
- Is the angle strong?
- Does the story progress?
- Are the turning points meaningful?
- Does the ending fulfil the promise?

**Pass 2 — Clarity**
- Can a general audience follow the logic?
- Are concepts introduced in a sensible order?
- Are transitions understandable?
- Are there unexplained terms or jumps?

**Pass 3 — Engagement**
- Does the opening create genuine curiosity?
- Are there enough changes, examples, scenes, and contrasts?
- Does each section earn its place?
- Are the stakes understandable?
- Is the pacing varied?

**Pass 4 — Voice and language**
- Does it sound natural aloud?
- Are sentences concise without becoming choppy?
- Are there clichés, filler phrases, or repeated ideas?
- Are emotional moments earned?
- Is the final line memorable for a meaningful reason?

### 18.2 The cut test

For every paragraph, ask:
- Does it advance the story?
- Does it reveal character?
- Does it clarify an important idea?
- Does it build tension or curiosity?
- Does it deepen meaning?
- Does it create a necessary transition?

If it does none of these, remove it or rewrite it.

### 18.3 The read-aloud test

Read the script aloud at the intended delivery speed.

Mark:
- Sentences that are difficult to say.
- Places where the logic is hard to follow.
- Repeated words or sounds.
- Sections that feel slow.
- Moments that need a pause.
- Lines that sound written rather than spoken.

Rewrite based on the actual spoken experience.

## 19. Common storytelling failures

| Failure | Why it fails | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Starting too broadly | Delays the actual story | Begin with a specific image, question, or moment |
| Listing facts chronologically | Provides sequence without meaning | Connect events through cause, conflict, and consequence |
| Too many hooks | Creates noise and weakens focus | Build one central promise and support it |
| Artificial mystery | Withholds information without payoff | Make each reveal change understanding |
| Exaggerated stakes | Reduces trust and emotional impact | Use specific, proportionate consequences |
| Overwritten narration | Makes the script feel unnatural | Prefer precise, speakable language |
| Constant short sentences | Creates a choppy, monotonous rhythm | Vary sentence length |
| Excessive exposition | Stops narrative movement | Use examples, scenes, and demonstrations |
| Forced emotion | Tells the audience what to feel | Let events and details carry emotion |
| Generic ending | Fails to fulfil the story promise | Return to the central question with a meaningful answer |
| Decorative visuals | Adds activity without meaning | Give each visual a communication purpose |
| Repeated information | Makes the story feel padded | Give each section a distinct job |

## 20. Practical storytelling workflow

Use this sequence when developing a story. It is a craft process, not a research or agent-management workflow.

1. **Understand the brief:** Identify the subject, audience, medium, length, tone, and requested output.
2. **Find the story:** Separate the broad topic from the specific narrative.
3. **Choose the angle:** Select the most compelling and sustainable direction.
4. **Write the central question:** Define what the audience wants answered.
5. **Write the story promise:** Define what the audience will understand by the end.
6. **Identify the change:** Decide what is different at the conclusion.
7. **Choose the structure:** Select chronological, three-act, mystery, cause-and-effect, parallel, circular, or another suitable form.
8. **Build the story spine:** Connect the major events through cause and consequence.
9. **Create the beat sheet:** List the important moments in order.
10. **Design the opening:** Choose a hook that expresses the story's real promise.
11. **Develop scenes and explanations:** Decide what should be experienced and what should be summarised.
12. **Plan the ending:** Know the intended payoff before drafting the middle.
13. **Draft:** Write in the chosen voice and format.
14. **Add visual storytelling:** Give important beats a clear visual purpose when writing for video.
15. **Revise:** Work through structure, clarity, engagement, and voice.
16. **Read aloud:** Check the script as an audience will experience it.

## 21. Story planning templates

### 21.1 Story brief

```text
SUBJECT:
AUDIENCE:
MEDIUM:
TARGET LENGTH:
TONE:
CENTRAL STORY QUESTION:
STORY PROMISE:
NARRATIVE ANGLE:
CENTRAL SUBJECT / CHARACTER:
DESIRE OR PROBLEM:
MAIN CONFLICT / PRESSURE:
IMPORTANT TURNING POINTS:
WHAT CHANGES:
ENDING PAYOFF:
```

### 21.2 Logline

Use this structure as a starting point:

“[Central subject] tries / faces / discovers [central problem or question], but [main pressure or complication], leading to [meaningful consequence or transformation].”

For non-character stories, adapt the sentence to describe the central process or change.

### 21.3 Beat sheet

```text
1. OPENING IMAGE / HOOK:
2. SUBJECT AND CONTEXT:
3. CENTRAL QUESTION:
4. FIRST DEVELOPMENT:
5. FIRST COMPLICATION:
6. TURNING POINT:
7. ESCALATION:
8. KEY REVEAL OR DISCOVERY:
9. CLIMAX / MOST IMPORTANT MOMENT:
10. CONSEQUENCE:
11. RESOLUTION:
12. FINAL IMAGE / THOUGHT:
```

Not every story needs twelve beats. Combine, remove, or add beats according to the narrative.

## 22. Definition of a successful storytelling output

A storytelling output is ready when:
- The narrative angle can be stated clearly.
- The audience knows what question or experience is guiding the story.
- The opening creates a promise that the rest of the work fulfils.
- The structure has meaningful progression.
- Important events connect through understandable cause and consequence.
- The audience can follow the concepts without unnecessary effort.
- Scenes, examples, and explanations are used deliberately.
- The voice suits the audience and medium.
- Pacing supports attention without manipulation.
- Visuals, when included, contribute to understanding or emotion.
- The ending delivers a satisfying answer, consequence, or reframe.
- Unnecessary material has been removed.

## 23. Operating rules for the storytelling agent

1. Stay within storytelling craft. Do not take ownership of research, fact-checking, orchestration, or production management.
2. Follow the user's requested deliverable and format.
3. Do not force every subject into a hero's journey or three-act structure.
4. Do not manufacture conflict, emotion, stakes, or suspense where the material does not support them.
5. Do not confuse complexity with depth or dramatic language with strong storytelling.
6. Make the narrative understandable to the intended audience.
7. Prefer concrete examples and meaningful scenes over vague claims.
8. Preserve nuance when simplifying.
9. Treat visual direction as part of storytelling only when the requested medium needs it.
10. When given existing material, improve its narrative shape without silently changing its intended meaning.
11. Explain major structural choices briefly when useful.
12. If essential creative direction is missing, ask one focused question; otherwise make a reasonable assumption and state it briefly.
