# Human Attention, Curiosity, Story Engagement & Content Retention

## An evidence-led, platform-independent reference manual for communicators and content creators

**Research cut-off:** 18 November 2026  
**Scope:** Human attention, curiosity, cognitive processing, narrative engagement, emotion, memory, motivation, trust and ethical persuasion.  
**Use case:** A practical reference for writing, video, documentaries, educational content, spoken communication, presentations and advertising.

---

## How to use this manual

This is an interdisciplinary evidence synthesis, not a systematic review or a claim that every possible paper has been searched. It prioritises peer-reviewed reviews, meta-analyses, foundational theories and influential experiments. Some cited articles were available through abstracts or bibliographic records rather than full text; where that limits interpretation, the text avoids claims beyond the available evidence.

Three labels are used throughout:

- **Established / well-supported:** supported by multiple studies, a substantial research tradition, or a high-quality review/meta-analysis. This still does not mean the effect is guaranteed in every context.
- **Context-dependent / developing:** plausible and supported in some settings, but effect size, mechanism or generalisability varies.
- **Creator heuristic:** a useful practical technique or label that should not be mistaken for a scientific law or a validated psychological construct.

The central distinction is this: **capturing attention is an event; retaining attention is a continuing exchange of value.** A striking opening may win an initial glance. Continued attention depends on whether the content remains understandable, relevant, sufficiently rewarding, and worth the effort.

# 1. Executive summary

Human attention is not one fixed resource with one universal duration. It is a family of processes: selecting information, maintaining a goal, resisting distraction, switching between tasks and allocating limited processing capacity. A sudden sound, movement, contrast or unexpected event can attract attention (bottom-up influence), while goals, interests, knowledge and expectations guide attention from the top down. Recent work treats these influences as interacting with selection history: what a person has learned to attend to before can also bias what stands out now (Theeuwes, 2025).

Curiosity is not created by withholding information indefinitely. It often arises when someone notices a meaningful gap between what they know and what they want to know. A usable curiosity gap must be both noticeable and tractable: the audience needs enough context to understand the question, and enough confidence that an answer is attainable. Newer accounts also emphasise learning progress: curiosity may persist when people sense that each step is helping them understand more, not merely when uncertainty remains unresolved (Loewenstein, 1994; Litman & Spielberger, 2003; _Curiosity and the dynamics of optimal exploration_, 2024).

Hooks are practical communication devices, not a single scientific taxonomy. Curiosity, surprise, contradiction, emotion, relevance, questions, demonstrations and story openings can all work by different mechanisms. Their usefulness depends on the audience, the subject, prior knowledge, the promise made and the quality of what follows. A hook that creates an expectation the content never fulfils damages trust.

The term **“pre-hook”** is best treated as creator-industry shorthand for the opening signal or material that precedes or helps deliver the main hook. In the research reviewed for this manual, it did not emerge as a standardised psychological construct with a validated definition or standalone evidence base. The underlying phenomena—salience, orienting, attentional capture, perceptual fluency and relevance—are studied, but they should not be collapsed into one supposed “pre-hook effect.”

People continue with narratives when they can follow the situation, care about the outcome, anticipate meaningful developments and receive progress. Narrative transportation involves attention, imagery and affect; identification and emotional engagement are related but not identical. A 2026 meta-analysis of 52 studies (N = 12,705) found that narrative engagement was positively associated with narrative-consistent attitudes, intentions and behaviours, while also showing that attention, understanding, emotional engagement and presence have different relationships with narrative outcomes (Yi et al., 2026).

Cognitive load matters. Working memory is limited, and the audience must use it to understand the content rather than decode clutter, confusing language, irrelevant details or mismatched visuals. Chunking, clear signposting, coherent visuals and well-paced segments can help. In multimedia learning, evidence supports principles such as coherence, signalling, contiguity and segmenting, but individual results depend on prior knowledge and task complexity (Sweller, 1988; Mayer, 2017).

Emotion can strengthen the encoding of central, emotionally important information, but it can also narrow attention and reduce memory for peripheral details. A high-arousal moment is not automatically a good learning or storytelling moment. Emotion should clarify what matters, not replace explanation (McGaugh, 2004; Waring & Kensinger, 2009).

Sharing, following, commenting and returning are different behaviours with different motives. People may share because content is useful, identity-relevant, emotionally activating, socially meaningful or conversation-worthy. Berger and Milkman (2012) found that high-arousal emotions such as awe, anger and anxiety were associated with greater sharing in their dataset and experiments; this is not a universal rule that creators should manufacture anger or anxiety.

Long-term trust is not the same as short-term attention. Trust is built by accurate claims, transparent uncertainty, fair framing, consistent delivery and respect for the audience's autonomy. Manipulative urgency, deceptive curiosity gaps and exaggerated promises may win a click while reducing the probability that the audience will believe the next promise.

## The practical principle

> **Make the audience notice something worth noticing; help them understand why it matters; make progress visible; answer the questions you create; and leave them with a worthwhile idea, feeling or next question.**

# 2. Foundations of human attention

## 2.1 Attention is a set of processes, not a single meter

| Process             | Meaning                                                                        | Content implication                                                                                           |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Selective attention | Prioritising some information while filtering or suppressing other information | Give the audience a clear focal point. Competing visual and verbal elements make selection harder.            |
| Sustained attention | Maintaining task-relevant attention over time                                  | Keep the task or story meaningful; provide structure and periodic progress. Do not assume one fixed duration. |
| Divided attention   | Trying to process multiple streams or tasks at once                            | Critical explanation should not compete with unrelated narration, animation, text or sound.                   |
| Attentional control | Maintaining a goal and regulating distraction                                  | Make the purpose and next step clear enough that the audience can keep following.                             |
| Attentional capture | A salient event receives priority, sometimes despite the person's current goal | Contrast, motion, novelty or abrupt change can attract attention, but do not necessarily create interest.     |
| Orienting           | Shifting attention toward a potentially relevant event                         | A visual or verbal cue can signal that something important has changed.                                       |

These processes overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A viewer can look at a bright object without understanding it; listen to a sentence while missing its meaning; or remain with a story because the goal and outcome matter to them.

## 2.2 Bottom-up and top-down attention

**Bottom-up influence** begins with features of the stimulus: brightness, contrast, motion, loudness, abrupt onset, colour difference or an unexpected change. These features can make an event stand out even when it is not relevant to the person's goal.

**Top-down influence** comes from the viewer's goals, knowledge, expectations, interests and current task. A person who wants to repair a bicycle will notice a broken chain faster than someone with no reason to look for one.

Theeuwes (2025) describes attentional control as involving three interacting influences: bottom-up, top-down and selection history. The third matters because previously rewarded or repeatedly selected features can gain priority. This means that attention is shaped not only by the current visual stimulus or stated goal, but also by what has been learned to matter before.

**Application:** Do not ask only, “How can I make this opening visually louder?” Also ask, “What is this audience already looking for, worried about, curious about or trying to understand?” Relevance often makes a modest cue more powerful than an elaborate but unrelated effect.

## 2.3 Salience, novelty and relevance

- **Salience** is how strongly something stands out relative to its surroundings and competing information.
- **Novelty** is newness relative to a person's experience or expectations.
- **Relevance** is the extent to which information matters to the person's goals, interests, identity or current question.
- **Personal significance** is relevance attached to the individual's own life, relationships, resources, identity or future decisions.

A salient event is not necessarily relevant; a novel event is not necessarily useful. A sudden explosion in a video can attract a glance, but if the viewer cannot see why it belongs, the interruption may feel like noise. A familiar subject can be compelling when presented with a surprising implication.

## 2.4 Attention limits and cognitive load

Attention and working memory are limited, but their limits are task-dependent. Nilli Lavie and colleagues' load-theory research found that high perceptual load can reduce processing of distractors, while high working-memory or cognitive-control load can increase distractor interference (Lavie et al., 2004; Lavie, 2010). In plain language: when people are already using their cognitive control heavily, it can become harder to ignore competing inputs.

Cowan's review argues that the capacity of working memory is often closer to a few chunks than to a universal “seven items” rule; chunk size and prior knowledge matter (Cowan, 2001). The popular “seven plus or minus two” line from Miller (1956) is frequently applied too broadly. Miller discussed distinct kinds of limits and explicitly warned against treating them as one underlying capacity.

**Practical consequences:**

1. Introduce unfamiliar terms before building several ideas on top of them.
2. Avoid putting a dense diagram, fast narration, subtitles, labels and music changes in competition at the same moment.
3. Use one primary idea per meaningful segment, while allowing familiar material to move faster.
4. Slow down at conceptual turning points, not necessarily at every sentence.
5. Assume different viewers bring different prior knowledge, language fluency, sensory needs and attention conditions.

## 2.5 Divided attention and media multitasking

“Multitasking” often means rapidly switching between tasks rather than fully processing both simultaneously. Reviews of media multitasking show mixed results across populations and tasks; some research finds poorer performance on certain attention or memory measures among heavier media multitaskers, while other studies report null or context-specific results (Uncapher & Wagner, 2018; Jeong & Hwang, 2016). Do not build a content strategy on the claim that all viewers are incapable of sustained attention. A more defensible conclusion is that competing tasks can make comprehension and recall harder, especially when the tasks are unrelated.

**Creator rule:** During the most conceptually demanding moments, reduce competing information. Movement should demonstrate, orient or reinforce the idea—not merely keep the screen busy.

## 2.6 Individual and contextual differences

Attention changes with age, fatigue, sleep, stress, interest, motivation, sensory conditions, expertise, task design and the environment. A recent meta-analysis of attention-control research also examines meaningful individual differences rather than treating attention control as identical across people (Unsworth, Miller & Strayer, 2024).

There is no useful universal rule such as “every viewer loses attention after X seconds.” The relevant design question is more specific: **What does this segment ask this audience to process, and what makes that processing worthwhile?**

# 3. Curiosity and information-gap psychology

## 3.1 Information-gap theory

Loewenstein's influential account describes curiosity as a response to a perceived gap in knowledge or understanding. The person must have enough knowledge to recognise what is missing. A question can become compelling when the viewer knows what they do not know and believes an answer may be available (Loewenstein, 1994).

A content creator can use this by establishing:

1. **Known information:** what the audience already believes or can quickly understand.
2. **Missing information:** the precise unresolved part.
3. **Importance:** why resolving it changes the meaning, decision or story.
4. **Answerability:** a credible sign that the content can actually resolve it.

Example: “We use the computer mouse every day. But why did its earliest version have a wooden shell and two wheels?” The audience understands the object, sees a specific gap and can anticipate a historical explanation.

## 3.2 Epistemic and perceptual curiosity

- **Epistemic curiosity** is a desire to acquire knowledge or understand how something works.
- **Perceptual curiosity** is interest triggered by sensory features or unusual stimuli.
- **Specific curiosity** focuses on resolving a particular question.
- **Diversive curiosity** is a broader search for novelty or stimulation.

Litman and Spielberger (2003) developed measures distinguishing aspects of epistemic curiosity and perceptual curiosity. These distinctions are useful because a strange visual may attract attention without creating a desire to learn, while a specific question can sustain attention after the novelty has faded.

## 3.3 Uncertainty: productive versus frustrating

Not all uncertainty creates useful curiosity. It can motivate exploration when:

- the subject is understandable;
- the missing information matters;
- the audience can form a plausible question;
- the next step appears achievable;
- each section provides some progress.

It can create frustration when:

- the audience does not know what the story is about;
- too many unknowns are introduced at once;
- the question feels irrelevant;
- the creator repeatedly promises an answer but delays it without adding insight;
- the eventual answer is trivial compared with the promise.

A 2023 study of work-related information gaps found a double-edged relationship: gaps could elicit both specific curiosity and frustration. Although workplace findings should not be directly equated with video viewing, they reinforce the practical point that an information gap is not automatically positive.

A 2024 review, _Curiosity and the dynamics of optimal exploration_, discusses uncertainty, information gain and learning progress, suggesting that curiosity may be sustained by the felt progress of learning rather than by uncertainty alone.

## 3.4 The curiosity progression model

This is a **creator heuristic**, not a validated universal sequence:

```text
Familiar anchor
      ↓
Specific anomaly or question
      ↓
Why the answer matters
      ↓
A clue, partial answer or new piece of context
      ↓
A better question
      ↓
Resolution that changes understanding
```

The important feature is progress. The audience should not feel that the creator is hiding the entire explanation behind an endless series of teasers.

## 3.5 Open questions and open loops

An **open question** is a question the content has intentionally not yet answered. It can create anticipation if the audience remembers it and expects a satisfying resolution.

An **open loop** is a creator's shorthand for a temporarily unresolved narrative or informational thread. It is not a guarantee of retention. The 2025 meta-analysis by Ghibellini and Meier found that the classic Zeigarnik memory advantage for unfinished tasks remains questionable and difficult to replicate, while evidence for a related tendency to resume interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect) may be more general. A task-resumption effect is not the same as a claim that every unresolved video question will hold viewers.

**Use open loops sparingly:** make the question clear, keep track of it, provide meaningful progress and close it. An unresolved thread that is abandoned is not suspense; it is a broken promise.

# 4. First impressions and hook psychology

## 4.1 What a hook does

A hook is a communication choice intended to make a person allocate further attention to a message. It may attract attention, establish relevance, raise a question, promise a benefit or introduce a situation. “Hook” is a practical creator term; the research does not establish one universal hook formula that works for all audiences and media.

An effective opening often performs several functions at once:

- It is noticeable enough to be perceived.
- It is legible enough to be understood.
- It is relevant enough to matter.
- It makes a credible promise about what follows.

A hook that attracts attention but does not clarify relevance can create a momentary interruption without sustained interest.

## 4.2 Hook types and the psychological mechanisms behind them

The following categories are useful writing and editing patterns, not mutually exclusive scientific types. Every example is illustrative rather than an empirically guaranteed formula.

| Hook type                     | Psychological mechanism                                           | What the audience notices / expects                | Why they may continue                              | When it fails                                            |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Curiosity gap                 | Perceived gap between current knowledge and desired understanding | A specific unknown; expects an explanation         | Wants to close the gap                             | Gap is vague, irrelevant or never resolved               |
| Surprise / incongruity        | Violation of an expectation or schema                             | Something does not fit what they believed          | Wants to update the mental model                   | Surprise is random or impossible to interpret            |
| Contradiction                 | Conflict between two claims or between belief and evidence        | A clash: “I thought X, but Y appears true”         | Wants to know which account is correct             | The contradiction is false, trivial or manufactured      |
| Question-based                | Explicitly directs thought toward a missing answer                | A clear question                                   | Wants an answer, especially if personally relevant | Generic question with no stakes or clear answer          |
| Emotional                     | Emotional salience, empathy, goal relevance                       | A human consequence, loss, hope, fear or delight   | Wants to understand what happened or what it means | Emotion is exploitative, mismatched or unearned          |
| Relatable situation           | Self-referencing and recognition                                  | A familiar problem or experience                   | Expects recognition, explanation or a solution     | Assumes everyone shares the same experience              |
| Problem / promise             | Goal activation and anticipated utility                           | A problem and a credible benefit                   | Expects the content to help                        | Promise is inflated or the solution is weak              |
| Unexpected fact               | Novelty, surprise and schema revision                             | A specific fact that changes the subject's meaning | Wants context and implications                     | Fact is unverified, stripped of context or merely trivia |
| Pattern interruption          | Attentional capture through a change in sensory pattern           | Sudden change in image, sound, pace or format      | May briefly orient and then evaluate relevance     | Repeated interruptions become predictable noise          |
| Story opening / in medias res | Narrative orientation, conflict and outcome uncertainty           | A scene already underway                           | Wants to know who, what, why or how                | The audience lacks enough context to follow              |
| Visual demonstration          | Perceptual evidence and concrete representation                   | A visible process, result or anomaly               | Wants to understand how or why it works            | Demonstration is decorative or not explained             |
| Direct value statement        | Goal relevance and expected utility                               | What they will learn, decide or be able to do      | Expects a useful payoff                            | It sounds generic or lacks proof                         |

## 4.3 The six questions to evaluate every hook

For each opening, ask:

1. **Mechanism:** What is doing the psychological work—novelty, relevance, curiosity, emotion, contrast, utility or story?
2. **Notice:** What will the audience actually perceive first?
3. **Expectation:** What answer, experience or benefit does the opening imply?
4. **Continuation:** What unresolved but answerable question makes the next moment worthwhile?
5. **Fit:** Does this fit the audience's prior knowledge, interests and context?
6. **Integrity:** Does the content fulfil the expectation without deception or unnecessary delay?

## 4.4 Examples and failure analysis

**Curiosity:** “The first computer mouse did not look anything like the one on your desk.”  
Works when followed by a visual comparison and an explanation of why the design changed. Fails if the difference is minor or the video never explains the evolution.

**Contradiction:** “The familiar version of this object was not the first version—and the earlier design solved a different problem.”  
Works if the history genuinely challenges a common assumption. Fails if the supposed myth was never commonly believed.

**Problem / promise:** “Why does this everyday device still have a feature inherited from an older technology?”  
Works when the answer provides historical and practical context. Fails if the feature is not meaningfully connected to the audience's question.

**Emotional / human story:** “One small design decision changed how millions of people interacted with computers.”  
Works if a real person, consequence or turning point supports the claim. Fails when the scale of impact is exaggerated.

**Visual demonstration:** Show a mechanical object operating, then freeze at the part most viewers overlook.  
Works when the visual makes an invisible mechanism easier to understand. Fails when it is an unexplained spectacle.

**Direct value:** “By the end, you will understand why this familiar design became the standard.”  
Works when the video actually explains the historical causes. Fails when the promise is too broad to verify.

## 4.5 Verbal, written and visual openings

- **Verbal opening:** The first spoken line should be easy to parse and connected to the topic. Avoid stacking multiple subordinate clauses before the point becomes clear.
- **Written opening:** A headline or first sentence should give a clear subject and a meaningful tension, benefit or question. Do not make the audience decode a deliberately obscure phrase.
- **Visual opening:** A visual can demonstrate the anomaly or consequence before narration explains it. The visual should answer “what am I looking at?” quickly enough for the explanation to land.
- **Sound opening:** A sound cue can orient or interrupt, but loudness alone is not meaning. A sound that has no relationship to what follows can feel like a cheap interruption.

## 4.6 Hook quality test

Rate each item from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). This is an editorial checklist, not a psychometric scale.

| Criterion        | Test question                                              |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Clarity          | Can a new viewer understand the subject quickly?           |
| Relevance        | Is there a reason this audience should care?               |
| Specificity      | Is the question or promise concrete?                       |
| Credibility      | Can the content support the claim?                         |
| Curiosity        | Is there something meaningful left to discover?            |
| Payoff potential | Can the rest of the piece deliver something satisfying?    |
| Honesty          | Does the opening avoid exaggeration and false implication? |

A high-scoring hook is not necessarily loud, fast or mysterious. It is an opening whose promise is both compelling and fulfilable.

# 5. Pre-hooks and attention capture

## 5.1 Is “pre-hook” a recognised scientific concept?

The term **pre-hook** did not emerge in the reviewed academic literature as a standardised construct with a widely accepted definition, validated measure or distinctive body of experimental findings. It is best understood as a creator / production term: an initial cue, image, sound, movement or statement that comes immediately before, or helps set up, the main hook.

That does not mean the opening seconds are unimportant. It means the science should be described using the constructs it actually studies: attentional orienting, salience, novelty, attentional capture, perceptual processing, relevance, expectations and goal-directed attention.

## 5.2 What can happen before the main hook?

1. **Detection:** a person notices a new image, sound, movement or text element.
2. **Orientation:** attention shifts toward it.
3. **Basic parsing:** the person begins to identify what is happening.
4. **Relevance judgement:** they decide whether the subject may matter to them.
5. **Expectation formation:** they infer what kind of content or payoff may follow.

This is a useful explanatory sketch, not a strict sequence. Some processes overlap, and people may reject or accept the content almost immediately.

## 5.3 Distinguish the parts of an opening

| Term                  | Practical meaning                                              | Main question it answers           |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Initial attention cue | First perceivable signal: frame, sound, motion, word or layout | “What made me notice?”             |
| Attention capture     | The shift of attention toward a salient event                  | “Did my attention turn toward it?” |
| Hook                  | Opening message that gives a reason to continue                | “Why should I stay?”               |
| Relevance             | Connection to the audience's goals, interests or knowledge     | “Why does this matter to me?”      |
| Context               | Information needed to understand the subject and question      | “What is happening?”               |
| Curiosity             | Motivation to resolve a meaningful unknown                     | “What do I want to find out?”      |
| Narrative setup       | Establishes situation, agent, stakes, obstacle or direction    | “What is at stake?”                |
| Payoff                | The promised answer, reveal, utility or emotional resolution   | “Was it worth continuing?”         |

## 5.4 Practical opening model

```text
FIRST SIGNAL
  A visible / audible / verbal cue worth noticing
       ↓
ORIENTATION
  The audience can identify the subject or situation
       ↓
REASON TO CARE
  The content establishes relevance, stakes or utility
       ↓
OPEN QUESTION / PROMISE
  The audience knows what it may discover
       ↓
EARLY PROGRESS
  The content begins paying off the promise
```

Do not treat this as a mandatory timing formula. A single image can capture attention and state the question; a strong story scene can provide context and stakes simultaneously. The correct order depends on the content and the audience.

**Important:** A pre-hook should not be an unrelated “attention hack.” If the first shot suggests a disaster, reveal, celebrity or shocking event that has no meaningful connection to the video, the audience may feel tricked once the real subject appears.

# 6. Cognitive processing and information delivery

## 6.1 Working memory and long-term memory

Working memory holds and manipulates information currently needed for a task. Long-term memory stores knowledge and experiences over time. Working memory is limited, while long-term knowledge can provide schemas that make new information easier to understand.

Sweller's cognitive load theory began by explaining how problem-solving demands can consume capacity needed to acquire schemas (Sweller, 1988). In content design, a viewer who is unfamiliar with the topic may need to process new terminology, the visual, the example and the causal explanation at once. This is a heavier task than it is for an expert.

## 6.2 Three useful forms of cognitive load

The classic cognitive-load framework distinguishes:

- **Intrinsic load:** difficulty inherent in the material and the number of elements that must be understood together.
- **Extraneous load:** difficulty created by poor presentation, confusing layout, unnecessary information or avoidable ambiguity.
- **Germane processing:** effort used to build or refine useful mental schemas. The terminology and interpretation of this category have evolved; use it as a teaching-design concept rather than a precise measurable bucket in every situation.

A creator cannot always remove intrinsic complexity. They can often reduce avoidable complexity by introducing concepts in a useful order, showing relationships clearly and removing distractions.

## 6.3 Chunking and segmentation

**Chunking** groups related pieces of information into a meaningful unit. A viewer may find “the three stages of the process” easier to remember than a list of unrelated details, provided the grouping reflects a real structure.

**Segmentation** divides a longer explanation into comprehensible parts. A segment should have a recognisable question, idea or transition—not merely a short time duration.

Practical methods:

- Preview a complex structure before explaining its parts.
- Use section labels that describe meaning, not vague labels such as “Part 2.”
- Introduce the name of an unfamiliar object before explaining its functions.
- Recap the relationship between ideas after a dense passage.
- Use a diagram when it clarifies spatial or causal relationships better than additional prose.

## 6.4 Multimedia learning

Mayer's review of multimedia-learning research summarises principles for reducing unnecessary processing and supporting learning (Mayer, 2017). These include:

- **Coherence:** remove material that does not support the learning goal.
- **Signalling:** highlight the important structure or relevant visual element.
- **Spatial contiguity:** put labels close to the relevant part of an image.
- **Temporal contiguity:** align narration with the visual explanation it describes.
- **Segmenting:** present complex material in manageable units.
- **Pre-training:** introduce the names and characteristics of important concepts before a complex explanation.
- **Modality:** spoken explanation alongside a relevant visual can sometimes be easier to process than forcing all information into dense on-screen text.

Boundary conditions matter. For example, the right amount of on-screen text depends on whether the audience needs captions, whether the narration is difficult to understand, the viewing environment and the learner's prior knowledge. “Never use subtitles” is not a valid universal rule.

## 6.5 Cognitive fluency

Processing fluency is the subjective ease with which information is perceived or understood. A review by Alter and Oppenheimer (2009) describes how fluency can influence judgements. Clear structure, legible typography, familiar language and well-matched visuals can make communication easier to process.

But ease is not the same as truth. Fluent statements can feel credible even when they are wrong. For factual content, fluency must be paired with evidence, careful qualification and transparent sourcing.

## 6.6 Repetition: useful reinforcement versus tedious duplication

Repetition can help when it serves a different cognitive function:

- first exposure introduces the idea;
- a later example shows how it works;
- a recap connects it to the larger argument;
- a retrieval prompt asks the audience to reconstruct it.

Repetition is wasteful when the same sentence is repeated without adding a new perspective, clarification or memory cue.

# 7. Narrative psychology and story immersion

## 7.1 Narrative transportation

Green and Brock (2000) described **transportation** as absorption in a story involving imagery, affect and attentional focus. Their experiments found that greater transportation was associated with stronger story-consistent beliefs and more favourable protagonist evaluations. This does not mean stories automatically persuade everyone or make false stories true; it shows that immersion can influence how audiences respond to narrative material.

A 2026 meta-analysis of 52 studies and 12,705 participants found positive associations between narrative engagement and narrative-consistent attitudes, intentions and behaviour. It distinguishes four dimensions—attention, understanding, emotional engagement and presence—and reports that these dimensions do not relate to every outcome in exactly the same way (Yi et al., 2026).

## 7.2 Identification, empathy and presence

- **Character identification:** the audience temporarily adopts or understands a character's perspective, goals or experience.
- **Empathy:** understanding or sharing something of another person's emotional state. Cognitive and affective empathy are related but different.
- **Presence:** the subjective sense of being in the represented situation or world.
- **Emotional engagement:** feeling concerned, moved, amused, anxious or otherwise emotionally involved.

These are not interchangeable. A documentary without a central human character can still be engaging through a clear question, object, process, conflict or historical mystery. A person can also feel immersed in a story without agreeing with its claims.

## 7.3 Conflict, stakes and change

A narrative needs some form of change, uncertainty or development. In a documentary, “conflict” need not mean a villain or a fight. It may be:

- a practical problem an inventor needed to solve;
- a design competing with alternatives;
- a discovery that challenged an existing belief;
- a technology that created an unintended consequence;
- a historical event whose outcome was uncertain;
- a trade-off between simplicity, cost, speed and usability.

**Stakes** explain why the change matters. They can be personal, social, technological, economic, cultural or intellectual.

## 7.4 Suspense and anticipation

Suspense is not merely “hide the answer.” It is a response that can arise when an audience has an unresolved question and cares about its outcome. Uncertainty can concern what will happen, how it will happen, when it will happen or whether it will happen. Lehne and Koelsch (2015) propose a domain-independent model in which conflict, instability, dissonance or uncertainty about emotionally significant future events can create a desire for resolution. They also distinguish tension from arousal: a surprising event may be highly arousing without necessarily producing suspense.

For nonfiction, suspense must stay within the bounds of evidence. The creator may delay a complete explanation to organise understanding, but should not imply that the historical record is more mysterious or uncertain than it is.

## 7.5 Story structure as a sequence of changing understanding

A useful, non-mandatory documentary structure is:

```text
QUESTION
  What are we trying to understand?
      ↓
CONTEXT
  What did people know / need / believe at the time?
      ↓
PROBLEM OR TENSION
  What made the situation difficult or uncertain?
      ↓
ATTEMPTS / EVIDENCE
  What was tried, discovered, rejected or changed?
      ↓
TURNING POINT
  What altered the direction of events?
      ↓
CONSEQUENCE
  What changed because of it?
      ↓
RESOLUTION
  What is the best-supported answer now?
      ↓
MEANING
  Why should the audience remember this?
```

This structure is a practical storytelling model, not a universal law. Expository content may be clearer with a direct explanation first; some stories are nonlinear; some discoveries are best understood through comparison rather than chronology.

# 8. Emotional investment

## 8.1 Emotion influences attention, interpretation and memory

Emotion can make information salient. McGaugh's review (2004) describes converging evidence that the amygdala modulates the consolidation of memories of emotionally arousing experiences. This does not mean every emotional moment is remembered equally or that invoking a brain region provides a content formula.

A common pattern is **memory narrowing**: people may remember the central emotional element better while remembering less about peripheral details. Waring and Kensinger (2009) review evidence for trade-offs between memory for emotionally arousing central items and neutral background information.

**Application:** If a scene is emotionally intense, make sure the most important information is also the central information. Do not place a crucial date, caveat or technical label in a minor corner while the audience's attention is focused on an emotionally charged image elsewhere.

## 8.2 Emotional contrast

Emotional contrast can create a perceivable change: confusion to understanding, uncertainty to relief, failure to success, isolation to connection, or expectation to surprise. Contrast is most effective when the states are causally related, not when emotions are randomly alternated to stimulate attention.

## 8.3 Empathy without emotional exploitation

Empathy can help the audience understand why an event matters to another person. It becomes exploitative when a creator uses suffering, fear or humiliation as decoration, withholds context, exaggerates harm or treats real people as props.

Use a person's story to illuminate the subject, not to manipulate the audience into accepting an unsupported conclusion.

## 8.4 Emotional intensity is not the same as quality

High-arousal emotion can increase sharing in some circumstances. Berger and Milkman's 2012 research found that awe, anger and anxiety were associated with greater virality than low-arousal sadness in their New York Times dataset, and experimental work supported an arousal-related role. But the result concerns transmission in particular contexts; it is not evidence that outrage is the best or most ethical content strategy.

# 9. Psychological momentum and retention

## 9.1 Retention is a series of renewed decisions

After the first moment, the audience repeatedly evaluates whether to continue. There may be no conscious decision at every second, but attention is continually allocated in response to relevance, effort, interest, understanding and expectation.

A useful editorial question for every segment is:

> What did the audience learn, feel, see or newly wonder about in this segment that makes the next one worthwhile?

## 9.2 Progression beats endless teasing

A content sequence should create forward movement. Each segment may:

- answer a sub-question;
- introduce evidence;
- reveal a consequence;
- complicate an earlier interpretation;
- show a failed alternative;
- resolve a tension;
- open a more interesting but connected question.

A sequence of teasers without information creates a debt that the content never repays. A sequence of facts with no causal connection feels like a list. Strong momentum combines **progress** with **anticipation**.

## 9.3 A question ledger

For a long video, documentary or article, maintain a simple internal table:

| Question introduced        | Why it matters          | Evidence / section that advances it | Where it is resolved      |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Main question              | Defines the whole piece | Major turning points                | Final synthesis           |
| Secondary question A       | Adds context or stakes  | Supporting sequence                 | Midpoint or later section |
| Secondary question B       | Explains a complication | Evidence or comparison              | Later section             |
| New question from a reveal | Deepens the story       | Next causal step                    | Explicit closure          |

Every major open question should be resolved, explicitly left uncertain for a defensible reason, or identified as a limitation in the evidence.

## 9.4 Pattern variation

A stable format helps the audience understand how to follow the content. Variation prevents predictability from becoming monotony. Change the mode when the idea calls for it:

- narration to demonstration;
- map to object close-up;
- broad context to a concrete person or event;
- explanation to comparison;
- question to answer;
- present-day object to historical origin.

Variation should mark meaning or support comprehension. Unrelated motion, random zooms and constant sound effects are not substitutes for narrative development.

## 9.5 The role of payoff

A payoff is the reward that makes an opening promise feel fulfilled. It may be:

- an answer to a question;
- a surprising but verified explanation;
- a useful procedure or decision;
- an emotionally satisfying resolution;
- a conceptual insight that reorganises earlier information.

A strong payoff often does two things: it answers the question and makes the audience reinterpret earlier information. It should not introduce an unsupported “twist” simply to create surprise.

# 10. Human motivation and engagement

## 10.1 Attention, engagement and action are different outcomes

| Outcome    | What it means                                                       | Example measure                                                   |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Attention  | A person notices or processes the content                           | Gaze, listening, task performance; platform proxies are imperfect |
| Engagement | A person becomes cognitively, emotionally or behaviourally involved | Comprehension, narrative engagement, thoughtful interaction       |
| Retention  | A person continues with the content or remembers it later           | Continued viewing / reading; later recall                         |
| Sharing    | A person passes the content or idea to someone else                 | Share, recommendation, conversation                               |
| Trust      | A person is willing to rely on the source or its claims             | Repeated credibility judgements and behaviour                     |
| Return     | A person chooses to engage again later                              | Repeat visits or voluntary follow-up                              |

A view, like or comment does not prove comprehension, trust or long-term loyalty. Each metric captures only part of a much larger behaviour.

## 10.2 Motivation: autonomy, competence and relatedness

Self-determination theory describes three basic psychological needs relevant to motivation: **autonomy**, **competence** and **relatedness** (Deci & Ryan, 2000).

- **Autonomy:** the person feels they have meaningful choice rather than being coerced.
- **Competence:** the person feels capable of understanding, learning or acting.
- **Relatedness:** the person feels connected to others or part of a meaningful social context.

Content can support these needs by offering clear choices, teaching progressively, acknowledging questions, avoiding humiliating the learner and building respectful communities. This theory is broad; it should not be reduced to three tactics guaranteed to increase watch time.

## 10.3 Why people share

People share content for multiple reasons: it is useful, entertaining, identity-expressive, socially relevant, emotionally moving, conversation-starting or important to someone they know. Berger and Milkman (2012) show that emotional arousal is one factor, not the only factor.

Practical questions:

- Does the content help someone explain something to a friend?
- Does sharing it express a value or identity?
- Does it answer a question the audience's community discusses?
- Is there a practical action or insight worth passing on?
- Would someone feel comfortable attaching their reputation to it?

## 10.4 Why people comment

A comment can be a response to a question, a disagreement, a correction, a personal story, a social invitation or a request for help. Comments are not always positive engagement. A creator should distinguish thoughtful conversation from conflict provoked by misleading framing.

## 10.5 Familiarity and return

Returning audiences often learn what kind of experience a source provides. A consistent editorial promise reduces uncertainty: the creator reliably explains a certain type of subject at a certain level of depth. Familiarity can make the audience more willing to invest attention, but it must be maintained through quality and variation rather than repetitive packaging.

# 11. Memory and recall

## 11.1 What makes information memorable?

Information is more likely to be retained when it is attended to, meaningful, organised, connected to existing knowledge and later retrieved. Emotion can strengthen memory for central details while reducing memory for peripheral ones. A vivid moment is not a guarantee that the audience remembers the factual explanation around it.

## 11.2 Distinctiveness and organisation

A distinctive example can make an abstract concept easier to retrieve. But too many unusual details compete with each other. Give the audience a memorable example that represents the central idea, then connect it explicitly to the explanation.

## 11.3 Retrieval practice

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) showed that taking memory tests can improve delayed retention compared with simply restudying prose, even when restudy can look better on an immediate test. This research comes from learning contexts, not entertainment retention, but it suggests useful educational-content techniques:

- ask the audience to predict before revealing an answer;
- invite them to recall the key reason after an explanation;
- use a short recap question;
- revisit an earlier concept in a later context.

The prompt should support learning, not act as an artificial interruption.

## 11.4 Make the central message retrievable

At the end of a piece, give the audience a concise synthesis:

- What was the original question?
- What evidence answered it?
- What changed in our understanding?
- What single idea should remain memorable?

A good conclusion is not merely a recap of every section. It identifies the relationship that ties the sections together.

# 12. Ethical persuasion and long-term trust

## 12.1 Credibility is part of the content experience

Source credibility research has a long history. Hovland and Weiss (1951) studied how source trustworthiness shaped communication effects. Later research has broadened the topic, but the practical lesson remains: audiences interpret information partly through beliefs about the communicator and their reasons for communicating.

For factual content, show how claims are supported. Separate:

- documented fact;
- a source's interpretation;
- your own inference;
- unresolved uncertainty;
- illustrative reconstruction.

Do not use confident delivery as a substitute for evidence.

## 12.2 Persuasion knowledge and reactance

Friestad and Wright's Persuasion Knowledge Model (1994) explains that people develop knowledge about persuasion attempts and use it to interpret and cope with them. When a creator's tactic becomes obvious, the audience may judge not just the message but also the creator's motives.

Psychological reactance is resistance that can occur when people feel their freedom or choices are threatened. A 2025 meta-analysis of health-communication studies found that high freedom-threatening language increased anger, negative cognitions and reactance on average, while also reporting substantial heterogeneity and moderators (Walter, 2025). This does not prove every forceful phrase backfires, but it supports avoiding coercive language as a default.

## 12.3 Ethical opening principles

- Do not imply a fact that the evidence does not support.
- Do not use a misleading thumbnail, title or opening scene to promise a different topic.
- Do not invent urgency, scarcity or danger.
- Do not exploit traumatic experiences for attention.
- Do not present a disputed explanation as settled consensus.
- Do not hide a material caveat until after the audience has acted.
- Make commercial relationships and persuasive intent clear when relevant.
- Close the curiosity gap with a real answer, not a rhetorical escape.

## 12.4 Short-term attention versus long-term trust

| Short-term tactic               | Possible short-term effect    | Long-term risk                                       |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Exaggerated claim               | More initial curiosity        | Disappointment and credibility loss                  |
| Artificial urgency              | Faster response               | Audience fatigue and reactance                       |
| Endless unanswered questions    | Temporary anticipation        | Frustration and abandonment                          |
| Outrage framing                 | Strong emotional reaction     | Polarisation, distrust and poor information quality  |
| Clear promise + accurate payoff | May be less sensational       | Builds predictable value and credibility             |
| Transparent uncertainty         | May reduce apparent certainty | Can strengthen trustworthiness and epistemic quality |

# 13. A revised universal content-design framework

The 13 stages in the original brief are useful prompts, but they should not be treated as a fixed psychological sequence. Some stages overlap; some are iterative; and some types of communication do not need all of them. The model below organises the task around the audience's continuing evaluation of **notice, understand, care, progress and payoff**.

## 13.1 Five interacting design jobs

### A. Win an opportunity for attention

Use perceptual clarity, meaningful contrast, novelty or a direct opening. This is not yet retention.

### B. Establish relevance and orientation

Identify the subject, audience connection, question, task or stakes. Give enough context to understand what is happening.

### C. Create a worthwhile information journey

Use clear questions, progressive evidence, understandable sequencing, causal links, emotional perspective or practical utility. Make progress visible.

### D. Deliver and resolve

Fulfil the promise, close important loops, explain what the evidence supports and give the audience a satisfying answer or result.

### E. Support memory, trust and voluntary return

Synthesize the main insight, provide retrieval cues, respect autonomy and leave a meaningful next horizon without manufacturing dependency.

## 13.2 Conceptual diagram

```text
              ┌──────────────────────────┐
              │  ATTENTION OPPORTUNITY   │
              │  signal / contrast / cue │
              └────────────┬─────────────┘
                           ↓
              ┌──────────────────────────┐
              │  RELEVANCE + ORIENTATION │
              │  subject / context / why │
              └────────────┬─────────────┘
                           ↓
              ┌──────────────────────────┐
              │  PROGRESSIVE EXPERIENCE  │
              │  question / evidence /   │
              │  story / utility / emotion│
              └────────────┬─────────────┘
                           ↓
              ┌──────────────────────────┐
              │  PAYOFF + RESOLUTION     │
              │  answer / result / meaning│
              └────────────┬─────────────┘
                           ↓
              ┌──────────────────────────┐
              │  MEMORY + TRUST + RETURN │
              │  synthesis / credibility │
              └──────────────────────────┘

   At every stage: check comprehension, cognitive effort,
   audience relevance, expectation and evidence quality.
```

| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |

# 15. Practical examples and worked cases

The following are **illustrative design exercises**, not controlled experiments or claims that these exact scripts have been scientifically validated.

## 15.1 Short documentary: the history of an everyday object

**Weak opening:** “Today we are going to talk about the history of the computer mouse.”  
It announces a topic, but gives no reason to care or question to resolve.

**Stronger opening concept:** Show an early wooden pointing device beside a modern mouse. Ask why the first design looked and worked differently. State that the video will explain the problem it was built to solve and how later designs changed.

**Progression:**

1. The familiar modern object and its everyday purpose.
2. The earlier computing problem that made a pointing device useful.
3. The original mechanism and its trade-offs.
4. Alternative designs and improvements.
5. The shift from specialist equipment to a familiar consumer object.
6. The modern consequence and what the design history reveals.

**Psychological principles used:** relevance, perceptual contrast, information gap, historical context, causal progression, visual demonstration and payoff.

**Integrity check:** Verify dates, inventors, prototypes and competing accounts. Do not imply one invention instantly created the modern product if the development was gradual.

## 15.2 Educational explainer: why an everyday process works

**Opening:** Present the observable result, then ask what mechanism causes it.  
**Orientation:** Name the process and explain the one or two concepts required to follow.  
**Progress:** Demonstrate the mechanism in stages, each with a labelled visual.  
**Retrieval:** Ask the learner to predict what would happen if one variable changed.  
**Payoff:** Explain the result and recap the causal model.

**Psychological principles used:** curiosity, pre-training, segmenting, signalling, multimedia learning and retrieval practice.

## 15.3 Written feature: challenging a familiar assumption

**Headline idea:** “The familiar version was not the original design. Here is the problem the earlier version was built to solve.”

**Lead:** Name the common assumption carefully. Offer a documented fact that complicates it. Give readers a map of what the article will establish.  
**Body:** Move from the earlier context to the design decision, evidence and consequences.  
**Ending:** Explain what the common assumption gets right, what it leaves out and what the evidence cannot establish.

**Psychological principles used:** contradiction, schema revision, clarity, cognitive fluency and trust.

## 15.4 Presentation: explaining a difficult proposal

Start with the decision the audience needs to make. State the recommendation. Then show the evidence, trade-offs, risks and implementation implications. Use one main message per slide and put detailed evidence close to the claim it supports. Finish by stating the decision requested and its consequences.

This format may work better with a direct conclusion than with a mystery hook. The purpose is informed decision-making, not suspense.

| Popular claim                                           | What the evidence supports more carefully                                                                                                                                          | Better rule                                                                                |

## 16.1 The 8-second myth in particular

The often-repeated claim that the average person has an eight-second attention span is not supported by a robust peer-reviewed measurement of a universal human capacity. The BBC World Service programme _More or Less_ traced the figure to a 2015 Microsoft Canada report, which cited the secondary site Statistic Brain; the programme could not find evidence for the underlying figure from the sources named. King’s College London’s Centre for Attention Studies likewise described the eight-second figure as a myth and noted that sustained attention depends on the task. Research can measure sustained attention for a defined task, but the result is task- and method-specific. “Attention span” should therefore not be treated as one number that applies to every viewer or type of content.

# 17. Research gaps and scientific limitations

1. **Lab-to-platform generalisation:** Laboratory tasks isolate mechanisms but do not fully reproduce real-world viewing, interruptions, recommendation systems or audience choice.
2. **Measurement:** Watch time, gaze, clicks, comprehension, recall, trust and emotional engagement are different outcomes. Platform metrics rarely measure all of them.
3. **Audience heterogeneity:** Age, language, culture, expertise, disability, prior knowledge and personal goals can alter how a message is processed.
4. **Cultural context:** Many studies use particular national or student populations. The same emotional cue, humour, metaphor or social identity signal may not generalise across cultures.
5. **Nonfiction versus fiction:** Narrative persuasion research does not automatically tell us how audiences evaluate documentary evidence or factual uncertainty.
6. **Causality:** Some correlations between media use, attention and behaviour cannot establish what caused what.
7. **Short versus long term:** Immediate engagement may not predict later memory, trust or voluntary return.
8. **Creator tactics:** Many popular editing practices, hook formulas and “retention hacks” lack direct controlled tests. They should be evaluated as creative hypotheses, not stated as scientific facts.
9. **Neuroscience translation:** A neural correlate does not automatically tell a creator what to write, how long to wait or which edit will work.
10. **Ethical limits:** Techniques that increase immediate compliance or arousal may harm autonomy, understanding or trust. Effectiveness alone is not a sufficient standard.

# 18. Glossary of key concepts

- **Attention capture:** A salient stimulus receives attentional priority.
- **Attentional control:** The ability to maintain task-relevant goals and regulate distraction.
- **Attentional orienting:** Shifting attention toward a stimulus or location.
- **Autonomy:** Experiencing meaningful choice and volition.
- **Bottom-up attention:** Attention influenced by stimulus features such as movement or contrast.
- **Cognitive dissonance:** Discomfort or tension arising from important inconsistency among beliefs, attitudes or actions.
- **Cognitive fluency:** The experienced ease of processing information.
- **Cognitive load:** The demands placed on limited cognitive processing capacity.
- **Cognitive empathy:** Understanding another person's perspective or mental state.
- **Divided attention:** Attempting to attend to multiple tasks or streams.
- **Epistemic curiosity:** Motivation to acquire knowledge or understanding.
- **Information gap:** A perceived difference between what is known and what is wanted to be known.
- **Intrinsic motivation:** Doing an activity because it is inherently interesting or satisfying.
- **Narrative engagement:** A multi-dimensional involvement with a story, including attention, understanding, emotional engagement and presence.
- **Narrative transportation:** Absorption into a story involving attention, imagery and affect.
- **Open loop:** A creator term for an intentionally unresolved question or narrative thread.
- **Perceptual curiosity:** Interest triggered by sensory properties or unusual stimuli.
- **Processing fluency:** Ease of perceptual, linguistic or conceptual processing.
- **Reactance:** Resistance that can arise when a message is experienced as threatening freedom or choice.
- **Relevance:** Connection between information and a person's goals, interests, needs or identity.
- **Retrieval practice:** Strengthening memory through actively recalling information.
- **Salience:** The degree to which an item stands out relative to competing information.
- **Selection history:** Influence of previous selections and learned priorities on current attention.
- **Sustained attention:** Maintaining attention on a task over time.
- **Top-down attention:** Attention guided by goals, expectations and prior knowledge.
- **Working memory:** Limited-capacity system for holding and manipulating information currently needed.

## Final operating checklist for creators

Before publishing, ask:

- Is the first cue noticeable and relevant, not merely loud?
- Can the audience identify the subject and the question?
- Is the promise specific, credible and worth the effort?
- Does every major section deliver information or emotional progress?
- Is the story understandable to someone without specialist knowledge?
- Are the most important details prominent during emotionally intense moments?
- Does each visual help the audience notice, understand, compare or remember something?
- Have all major open questions been answered or honestly qualified?
- Does the conclusion explain what the audience should remember?
- Would the audience still trust the creator after checking the claims?

**The goal is not to keep attention at any cost. It is to make continued attention worthwhile.**
