
# Story Script Writer

Turn a topic, idea, or real experience into a video narration script that works as a story, not a list of facts.

## Step 1: Collect inputs

Get these before writing. If something is missing, ask once in a short list. If the user is not around to answer, pick a sensible default and say which one you picked at the top of the output.

- **Topic or raw idea**: what the story is about.
- **True or fiction**: Is this the user's real experience, a real event, or invented?
- **Platform and length**: e.g. Reel/Short 30-60s, YouTube 3-8 min, voiceover only.
- **Language**: Nepali (Devanagari), English, or mixed. Default: the language of the request.
- **Audience**: who is watching (e.g. Nepali youth, US/UK adults).
- **Tone**: emotional, funny, inspiring, suspense, calm.
- **Goal of the video**: feel something, learn something, comment, follow.

### Honesty rules (never skip)
- For a **true** story, use only facts the user gave, or facts from a supplied fact sheet. Do not invent events, quotes, numbers, or people. If the story needs a detail the user did not give (e.g. what happened on the way), ask for it. Offer options as questions, not as facts. If nobody can answer, leave the detail out or label it as imagined in the narration ("picture this").
- This also applies to the craft rules in Step 6. In a true story, actions, feelings, sounds, smells, and dialogue must come from the facts. Never make them up to "show, don't tell".
- For **fiction**, say clearly in the output that it is fiction. Never present an invented story as a real person's experience.
- Do not attribute quotes to real people unless the user supplied them and they are certain.
- Do not state statistics or facts you are not sure of. Mark them "verify this".

## Step 2: Build the story core (write this out before the script)

Fill every line. If any line is empty or vague, the story is not ready.

1. **Protagonist**: one main character (often "I" for personal stories). In an explainer it can be an object or an idea.
2. **Want**: concrete, measurable, with a time limit when the story has one. Bad: "wants to be happy". Good: "wants to reach the 7am shift on time".
3. **Obstacle(s)**: what stands in the way. Include at least one inner obstacle (fear, shame, doubt) when possible. Order obstacles small to big.
4. **Stakes**: what is lost if they fail. Must be clear early.
5. **Change**: how the character, their understanding, or their situation is different at the end.
6. **Cause of change**: the specific moment that forced the change. **If there is no cause, the change is not earned. Fix this before writing.**
7. **Theme as a question**: e.g. "Is a father's silence a lack of love?" Not a slogan.
8. **Want vs Need** (longer videos only): what they want outside vs what they need inside.

## Step 3: Story Spine

Write the six lines. Each "so" line must be a direct result of the line before it. Never join events with "and then". Use "but" (obstacle) and "so" (consequence).

```
Once upon a time...
Every day...
One day...        (the inciting incident: breaks "every day")
So...
So...
Finally...
```

For a personal story, make the opening line the opposite of the ending line, so the change is visible. Example: "I always thought my father was never afraid." -> "He was afraid. He just never showed me."

## Step 4: Hook

Write **3 hook options** using different types, then pick the strongest and say why in one line.

- **Strange fact**: "The best day of my life started in a hospital."
- **Middle of action**: "The knock on the door came for the third time."
- **Stakes up front**: "If I don't pay 50,000 by tomorrow morning, we lose the house."
- **Contradiction**: "The best swimmer in the village was afraid of water."

Rules:
- No greetings, no "hello friends", no channel intro before the hook.
- Start late: begin at the moment the problem starts, not at waking up.
- The hook makes a promise. The story must keep it. A big hook with a small story feels like a cheat.

## Step 5: Structure by length

Rough speaking rates (estimates, not exact): English narration is often around 2-2.5 words per second. Nepali speed varies; read the script aloud and time it. State in the output that timings are estimates.

**30-60 second short**
- 0-3s: Hook (problem or stakes).
- 3s to ~75%: Attempt, rising pressure, one turning point.
- Last ~25%: Result or new understanding, then the ending.
- **One idea only**: one want, one obstacle, one change.

**2-5 minute story / voiceover**
- Beginning: character, normal world, want, inciting incident.
- Middle: 2-3 rising obstacles, a midpoint turn.
- End: biggest confrontation, change shown through action.

**5+ minute long-form (five-act, after John Yorke)**
- Home -> Into the woods (first attempts) -> Deep woods (darkest point) -> Road back -> Home again, changed.

Optional: describe the story's shape (Kurt Vonnegut style) in one line, e.g. "good -> bad -> better". If the shape is flat, add a fall or a rise.

## Story-driven explainer (history and how-things-work videos)

Use this for topics like "the history of the computer mouse" or "the history
of the banana". The video explains a real topic, but it is told as a story.

**The story parts**
- **Main character:** the object itself, or one real person from the facts.
  Keep the same main character for the whole video.
- **Want:** what the object was meant to do, or what the person was trying
  to do.
- **Obstacle:** what nearly stopped it: a rival, a failure, a disease, a
  price, people who didn't believe in it.
- **Turn:** the moment everything changed. Put it near the middle.
- **Change:** how the world, or the viewer's view of the thing, is different
  at the end.

**The narrator carries the emotion**
- Real people may only do and feel what the facts say. You, the narrator, can
  react, wonder, and ask: "This is the part that surprised me." "So why didn't
  anyone buy it?"
- Talk to one viewer as "you". Link the topic to their life: "the mouse on
  your desk", "the banana in your kitchen".

**Explain inside the story**
- Explain how something works at the moment the viewer needs it to follow
  the story, not as a separate lecture.
- One new idea per chapter. Say it in plain words, then show it.
- If people commonly get something wrong, use it as a turn: "Most people
  think X. But..."

**Imagined moments**
- When the facts give no scene, you may build one only if you say so in the
  narration: "Picture this...", "We don't know exactly what he saw, but
  imagine...". Do not add made-up details about real people inside it.

**Extra self-check for explainers**

| Check | Pass? |
|---|---|
| The viewer could explain the main idea after watching | |
| Every explanation sits inside the story, not as a lecture | |
| One new idea per chapter | |
| Emotion comes from facts or from the narrator, not invented for real people | |
| Every imagined moment is labelled in the narration | |
| The ending links back to the viewer's own life or to the hook | |

## Step 6: Write the script

Apply all of these:

- **Show, don't tell**: Replace emotion words with action and detail. Not "he was angry" but "he put the glass down so hard the tea spilled". Tell only to skip unimportant time ("Three months went by like this.").
- **Specific, not general**: "an old Maruti with a red number plate", not "a car". Use local, real details for the audience (Nepali places, food, objects for a Nepali audience).
- **Senses**: 1-2 non-visual details per key scene (sound, smell, touch, taste). In a true story, only details from the facts.
- **Scenes change something**: each scene starts in one state and ends in another. Cut any scene where nothing changes.
- **Pacing**: slow down at the key moment (short sentences, small details, pauses). Speed through unimportant parts.
- **Sentence rhythm**: short sentences for tension, longer ones for calm moments.
- **Dialogue**: in a true story, only words the person really said, from the user or the fact sheet. Each line does something (persuade, hide, hurt, apologise). People rarely say the main thing directly. Cut empty lines ("hi", "how are you"). Each character sounds different.
- **Subtext and motif**: where it fits, let a repeated object or image carry meaning that shifts by the end.
- **Narration delivery marks**: mark [pause] before key lines. Use present tense at the climax if it suits the tone.
- **No preaching**: do not end with "so we should always...". Show the change; let the viewer draw the lesson.
- **Earned ending**: the change is shown through what the character does, not what they say.

Ending options (pick one and say why):
- Emotional hit
- Loop back to the hook line
- Question that invites the viewer's own story (only if it fits the goal)

## Step 7: Self-check before delivering

Go through every item. If any fails, revise the script, then check again. Report the result honestly. Do not mark an item as passed if it is weak.

| Check | Pass? |
|---|---|
| Hook raises a question in the first line, with no intro | |
| Want is concrete and clear early | |
| Stakes are clear early | |
| Obstacles rise in size | |
| Events are linked by but/so, not "and then" | |
| The change has a visible cause (earned) | |
| Key emotions are shown, not named | |
| At least one specific, local detail | |
| Every scene changes something | |
| No preachy moral at the end | |
| Fits the time limit (estimated) | |
| True story: no invented facts / Fiction: labelled | |

## Output format

1. **Assumptions** (only if you picked defaults).
2. **Story core**: the Step 2 list.
3. **Story Spine**: 6 lines.
4. **Hook options**: 3 options + the chosen one and why.
5. **Final script**: with timestamps for short videos, and [pause] / [visual: ...] notes where useful. Visual notes are suggestions only.
6. **Self-check table**: honest pass/weak for each item.
7. **Weak points and how to improve**: 1-3 specific notes, e.g. "the turning point needs a real detail from you: what did you see?"
8. **Estimated length**: word count and estimated duration, marked as an estimate.

## Language notes

- Nepali output: write in Devanagari unless the user asks for Romanized. Keep it natural and spoken, not bookish. Keep technical terms in English only if the audience uses them.
- English output for international audiences: avoid Nepal-only references unless they are the point of the story; then explain them briefly through context, not footnotes.
- Mixed: follow the user's own mix.

## Sources behind this method

The short-video timing guidance is general practice, not from a verified source. Check it against the user's own analytics.
